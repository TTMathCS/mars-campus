Inside Mars

What lies under the surface, from the marsquakes recorded by NASA's InSight lander and from orbiters that measured Mars' gravity and magnetism: a crust, a mantle of rock, a core of liquid iron, and a magnetic field that died long ago.

Listening to MarsA seismometer on the ground

NASA's InSight lander set down on 26 November 2018 in Elysium Planitia, near the equator, and placed a seismometer on the ground. Until dust on its solar panels ended the mission in December 2022, it recorded 1,319 marsquakes.

Quakes send waves through a planet. They bend and bounce where the rock changes, and the slower shear waves cannot pass through liquid at all, so the timing of the echoes maps the layers inside, as seismometers have mapped the Earth for a century. InSight did this for another planet for the first time.

InSight also carried a heat probe meant to hammer itself 5 m into the ground. The soil was too cohesive for it to dig, and it was given up in 2021, so the heat flowing out of Mars' interior is still unmeasured.

Orbiters add the rest: they track how Mars' gravity tugs on them, which shows where the crust is thick or thin, and how strongly the planet wobbles as it turns, which shows how its mass is spread inside.

The layersCrust, mantle, a molten layer and the core

Crust. At InSight's landing site it is either about 20 or about 39 km thick (the quakes fit both); across the planet it averages 24 to 72 km, thicker under the southern highlands and thinner under the northern plains. Earth's crust is about 7 km under the oceans and 35 km under the continents.

Mantle. Hot rock, mostly olivine and pyroxene. Its cold, rigid top, together with the crust, forms a single shell 400 to 600 km thick. Earth's shell is broken into moving plates; Mars' is one piece, which is why its volcanoes stayed over the same hot spots and grew so tall.

A molten layer. Studies published in 2023 found that the bottom of the mantle, about 150 km thick, is molten rock. The first analysis had taken its top for the top of the core.

Core. Liquid iron, mixed with lighter elements such as sulfur, oxygen, carbon and hydrogen. Its radius is about 1,650 km, roughly half of the planet's; the first estimate, before the molten layer was recognised, was 1,830 km. Mars is less dense than Earth overall, 3.93 grams per cubic centimetre against 5.51, because it has less iron.

MagnetismA field that died

Earth's churning iron core makes a magnetic field round the whole planet. Mars has none today. But in 1999 Mars Global Surveyor found that old crust in the southern highlands is strongly magnetised, in bands hundreds of kilometres long: Mars once had a field, which froze into the rock as it cooled. Its dynamo stopped about four billion years ago.

Without a field, the wind of particles from the Sun strips gas from the top of the atmosphere. NASA's MAVEN orbiter has measured that loss since 2014 and concluded that much of Mars' early, thicker air was lost to space this way, which helped turn a planet with rivers and lakes into a cold desert. The lack of a field also lets radiation reach the ground: see hazards.

MarsquakesSmaller and fewer than Earth's

The largest marsquake InSight recorded, on 4 May 2022, was magnitude 4.7. Earth has thousands of quakes that size every year. Mars has no moving plates; its quakes come from faults in a planet that is slowly cooling and shrinking, and many come from Cerberus Fossae, a set of young cracks in Elysium Planitia.

InSight also felt meteorites strike. On 24 December 2021 a magnitude 4 quake turned out to be an impact that dug a crater 150 m wide in Amazonis Planitia and threw out blocks of ice: see hazards.

Still alive inside?Signs of a planet not quite dead

Most of Mars' volcanoes have been quiet for hundreds of millions of years. But some lava flows in Elysium Planitia are only a few million years old, young for a planet; the quakes of Cerberus Fossae come from the same region; and studies of its gravity and heights suggest that a plume of hot mantle may still rise beneath it. Mars may still erupt, very rarely.
