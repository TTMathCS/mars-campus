Weather

Thin, cold and dusty air: wind that barely pushes, dust devils 20 km high, storms that wrap the whole planet, clouds, frost and snow, and seasons twice as long as Earth's.

The airLess than 1% as thick as Earth's

What it is made of

Carbon dioxide95%
Nitrogen2.6%
Argon1.9%
Oxygen0.16%

The air presses down with 636 Pa on average, 0.6% of Earth's air pressure, and has about 1.6% of its density. There is almost no water vapour in it, about 0.02%, and almost no oxygen.

Why there is no liquid water

At about 600 Pa, the triple point of water, ice turns straight into vapour, and any water that melts boils away soon after. Liquid water can only last briefly in the lowest places, like the bottom of Hellas.

The air is still useful: its carbon dioxide can be made into oxygen and fuel, and its nitrogen and argon can dilute the oxygen inside a building, as nitrogen does on Earth. See resources.

ColdWarm afternoons, deadly nights

Thin air holds little heat. A summer afternoon near the equator reaches about 20 °C at the ground, and the same night falls below −80 °C. The average over the planet is −63 °C. Polar winter nights reach −125 °C, cold enough for carbon dioxide to freeze out of the air as frost. Each sol, the air near the ground warms and cools by about 60 °C, and the ground itself by more.

WindFast, but weak

The push of wind goes with the air's density times the speed squared. With air 1.6% as dense as Earth's, a 30 m/s storm wind, about 100 km/h, presses no harder than a 13 km/h breeze at home. It still lifts dust and moves sand: orbiters have watched sand dunes and ripples creep across the ground.

Dust devilsWhirlwinds 20 km high

On spring and summer afternoons the sun-warmed ground sets whirlwinds spinning, and their dark tracks criss-cross the plains in photographs from orbit. One photographed in 2012 in Amazonis Planitia, next to Arcadia, stood about 20 km high and only some 60 m wide.

Their gusts have swept rovers' solar panels clean, giving Spirit and Opportunity years of extra life.

Dust stormsSome cover the whole planet

Local dust storms rise every year, mostly in the southern spring and summer, when Mars is nearest the Sun. Every few Mars years one grows to cover the whole planet for weeks. Mariner 9, the first spacecraft to orbit Mars, arrived in one in 1971 and had to wait for the dust to settle before it could map the surface. In 2018 a global storm darkened the sky so much that the Opportunity rover's solar panels could no longer keep it alive, after 14 years of work.

Dust in the air also colours the sky: butterscotch by day, and blue round the Sun at sunset (see the surface).

Clouds, fog, frost and snowWater and dry ice

Thin clouds of water ice form over the volcanoes and the poles, and much higher up, clouds of carbon-dioxide ice. Fog fills canyons in the morning. Viking 2 photographed frost on the ground in winter at 48° N. The Phoenix lander's laser, at 68° N, watched snow falling from clouds at night, and on the poles carbon-dioxide snow falls in winter.

SeasonsTwice as long, and the air itself comes and goes

Mars tilts 25°, close to Earth's 23°, so it has seasons, each about twice as long as Earth's. Its orbit is more oval than Earth's, so it gets 45% more sunlight at its nearest to the Sun than at its farthest: the southern summer is short and hot, the northern one long and mild.

Each winter so much carbon dioxide freezes onto the pole that the air pressure over the whole planet drops by about a quarter, and returns in spring when the frost turns back into gas.
