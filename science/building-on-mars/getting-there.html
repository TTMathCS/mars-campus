Getting there

Moving people and materials from Earth to Mars: when to go, how long it takes, why landing is the hard part, what is worth carrying, and what is better made on Mars.

When to goOnce every 26 months

Earth and Mars line up for an efficient trip once every 26 months. The cheapest path is half an ellipse round the Sun, about 260 days; most missions take six to nine months. Miss the window and the next one is two years away, so everything for a settlement arrives in waves.

The window also sets the way home: a crew that lands has to wait about a year and a half on Mars for the planets to line up again, or take a much longer path back.

LandingThe hard part

The air of Mars is thick enough to burn up a ship but too thin to slow it gently, so heavy landers must finish on rockets. The heaviest landed so far, Perseverance, weighs about a tonne; it came down under a parachute and was lowered on cables from a rocket-powered crane.

A settlement needs loads of a hundred tonnes. SpaceX's Starship is designed to land them, braking in the air and then on its engines, after being refuelled in Earth orbit on the way. It has not yet flown to Mars.

What to bringBring little, make the rest

From Earth

  • Machines and robots, and their spare parts
  • Electronics, computers and solar cells
  • Nuclear reactors and their fuel
  • Medicines, seeds and starter cultures
  • Anything precise or rare

Made on Mars

  • Water, from ice
  • Air: oxygen from carbon dioxide and water, nitrogen and argon from the air
  • Rocket fuel: methane and oxygen
  • Building materials: sintered soil, sulfur concrete, glass
  • Later, iron and steel from the iron oxides in the soil

Engineers call making things on the spot in-situ resource utilisation. Fuel is the biggest saving: a ship fuelled on Mars does not have to carry the fuel for its return from Earth. See resources and rocket fuel.

Robots firstA home ready before the people

Robots land in the windows before the people. They dig, build and start the machines, so that air, water and power are running, and a stock of fuel is made, before a crew arrives. Rovers and haul trucks move soil and ice; every task they do is a task no one has to do in a suit.
