Living on Mars

Getting thereWhat to bring, and what to make on Mars

Windows. Earth and Mars line up for an efficient trip once every 26 months. The cheapest path is half an ellipse round the Sun, about 260 days; most missions take six to nine months.

Landing is the hard part. The air is thick enough to burn up a ship but too thin to slow it gently, so heavy landers must finish with rockets. The heaviest landed so far, Perseverance, weighs about a tonne. A settlement needs loads of a hundred tonnes: SpaceX's Starship is designed to land them, braking in the air and then on its engines, refuelled in Earth orbit on the way. It has not yet flown to Mars.

So you bring what Mars cannot make, such as machines, electronics, reactors, medicines and seeds, and make the heavy, simple things there: water, air, rocket fuel, bricks and glass, and later metals from the iron in the soil. Engineers call it in-situ resource use. Fuel is the biggest saving: a ship fuelled on Mars does not have to carry its fuel home from Earth.

Robots go first. They land in the windows before people, dig, build and start the machines, so that air, water and power are running when the crew arrives.

BuildingWith Mars soil, and under it

What Mars soil is: crushed basalt, grains of olivine, pyroxene and feldspar, with rusty iron oxides that make it red, and salts such as sulfates and perchlorates.

Ways to build with it, all tested on Earth with copies of Mars soil:

  • Sintering: heat the soil to about 1,100 °C and its grains fuse into a hard ceramic, with microwaves, lasers or focused sunlight.
  • Sulfur concrete: Mars soil has sulfur. Melted at about 130 °C and mixed with soil, it sets into a concrete that needs no water; tests reached about 50 MPa, as strong as good concrete.
  • 3D printing: NASA's 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge (2015–2019) ended with robots printing habitats; the winner, MARSHA by AI SpaceFactory, used basalt fibre in a plant-based plastic.
  • Burying: soil on top is the simplest shield of all.

The real load is the air inside. A building at 70 kPa pushes up on each square metre of roof as hard as 7 tonnes would press down on Earth. Soil balances it: at Mars gravity a metre of soil (1.7 t/m³) presses down with 6.3 kPa, so 11 m of soil balances 70 kPa, and 16 m balances a full Earth atmosphere.

The cold: a house in frozen ground must not melt the ice it stands in, so its walls are insulated, as buildings on permafrost are in Canada and Alaska.

WaterMined from ice, then used again and again

Where it is: as ice, at the poles and under the ground of the middle latitudes. In Arcadia Planitia radar shows clean ice tens of metres thick close to the surface.

Two ways to get it out. Dig ice-rich soil and bake the water out in a sealed oven; or drill into clean ice and melt a pool deep down, a "Rodwell", as the South Pole station has done for its water for years.

It takes energy: warming a tonne of ice from −60 °C and melting it takes about 130 kWh (our sum: 2.1 kJ per kg and degree to warm it, 334 kJ per kg to melt it).

Cleaning: Mars soil carries perchlorate salts, about half a percent at the Phoenix landing site, harmful to people; they are taken out by reverse osmosis or ion exchange before anyone drinks.

Recycling: the International Space Station now recovers 98% of its water, from washing, breath and urine. A Mars house recycles the same way, so the ice mine only tops it up.

AirOxygen from carbon dioxide and from water

A person uses about 0.8 kg of oxygen a day and breathes out about 1 kg of carbon dioxide.

From Mars air. MOXIE, a box the size of a car battery on the rover Perseverance, split carbon dioxide into oxygen and carbon monoxide at about 800 °C. From 2021 to 2023 it made 122 g of oxygen in 16 runs, up to 12 g an hour at 98% purity: the first oxygen made on another planet. Scaled up many times over, the same method could make the oxygen for people and for a rocket's trip home.

From water. Electrolysis splits water into hydrogen and oxygen: 1 kg of water gives 0.89 kg of oxygen.

The rest of the air. Pure oxygen burns too easily, so nitrogen and argon, 4.5% of Mars air, are separated out and added, as nitrogen fills Earth's air. Plants help too: a lit garden turns carbon dioxide back into oxygen.

FoodGrown indoors, under lamps

Why indoors: Mars gets 43% of Earth's sunlight and dust cuts it further; plants also need warmth, air pressure and shelter from radiation. So Mars farms are greenhouses, or rooms underground lit by LEDs, which can give plants exactly the light they use.

Mars soil can grow plants once fixed. It has no organic matter and little usable nitrogen, and its perchlorate salts must be washed out first. In experiments, crops grew in copies of Mars soil with nutrients added.

Most growing is soilless: hydroponics, roots in flowing water with nutrients, gives the highest yields. NASA's crop studies found that about 40 to 50 m² of high-yield crops under strong light can feed one person.

Light is the big cost. Our estimate: crops under strong light take about 400 µmol of light a second on each square metre, 16 hours a day, and LEDs make about 3 µmol per joule, so 45 m² takes about 6 kW while the lamps are on, about 100 kWh a day for each person fed.

Fish, insects and algae add protein; stores carry people through emergencies.

EnergyWhy the house runs on fission

Sunlight is 43% of Earth's above the air. Solar panels need dusting, give nothing in the 12-hour night, and a global dust storm can cut them to a few percent for weeks: the 2018 storm ended the Opportunity rover.

Wind is weak: with air 1.6% as dense as Earth's, a turbine makes about a sixtieth of the power it would at home in the same wind.

Fission works day and night, in any storm. NASA and the US Department of Energy tested KRUSTY, a small space reactor, in 2018, and NASA is developing 40 kW fission reactors for the Moon and Mars. Curiosity and Perseverance already run on nuclear heat, from plutonium generators of about 110 W.

Heat matters as much as electricity on a cold planet, and a reactor's waste heat can warm a house.

RadiationShielding with soil and water

On the ground Curiosity measured about 0.64 mSv a day, and 1.8 mSv a day on the way. Six months each way and 500 days on Mars add up to about 1 sievert, more than NASA's career limit of 600 mSv.

Light atoms shield best: water, plastics and soil stop the particles without making many new ones. On the way, ships can put their water and food round a shelter for solar storms.

On the ground, soil: a few metres remove most of the dose, and 16 m bring it below what people receive on Earth.

Rocket fuelMethane and oxygen from ice and air

A ship refuelled on Mars can fly home or on. Methane and oxygen were chosen for Starship partly because both can be made on Mars, as Robert Zubrin and David Baker proposed in their Mars Direct plan of 1990. The chemistry is more than a century old; a Sabatier reactor has made water from waste carbon dioxide on the Space Station since 2010.

2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂ (electrolysis)
CO₂ + 4 H₂ → CH₄ + 2 H₂O (Sabatier, nickel catalyst, about 300 °C)
overall: CO₂ + 2 H₂O → CH₄ + 2 O₂

Talking to EarthMinutes away, by radio and laser

Radio takes 3 to 22 minutes each way, depending on where the planets are, so there are no phone calls: messages, video letters and email. Every 26 months Mars passes behind the Sun as seen from Earth and contact drops for about two weeks.

Orbiters relay the rovers' data today. Lasers can carry far more: in 2023 NASA's DSOC experiment on the Psyche spacecraft sent high-definition video to Earth by laser from 31 million km away.
