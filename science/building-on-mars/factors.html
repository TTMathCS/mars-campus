Every factor, and the answer

Seventeen conditions a building on Mars, and the people in it, must stand up to, each with what Mars does and how engineers answer it. The conditions are measured: see weather and hazards.

FactorOn MarsThe answer
Thin air636 Pa, 0.6% of Earth's: far below the 6.3 kPa at which water boils at body temperatureSealed buildings and airlocks; full pressure suits outside. The air pushing out is a building's biggest load, so soil on top balances it
Air you can't breathe95% carbon dioxide, 0.16% oxygenMake oxygen from carbon dioxide or from water, add nitrogen and argon from the Mars air, and scrub out the carbon dioxide people breathe out
Cold−63 °C on average; −125 °C on polar winter nightsThick insulation, and the waste heat of the power plant to warm the rooms
Daily swingsThe air went from −89 to −31 °C in one sol at Viking 1, and the ground swings widerJoints, seals and glazing made to expand and shrink every sol, for decades
Wind17–30 m/s in dust storms, but in air 1.6% as dense as Earth'sSmall wind loads; shield surfaces, machines and seals from blowing sand
DustAs fine as smoke, clings by static, carries perchlorateAirlocks with dust rooms, or suitports that leave the suit outside; tight seals, filters and cleaning
Dust stormsWeeks of dim light, now and then over the whole planetPower that does not need the Sun; stores of food, water and spare parts
Weak sunlight43% of Earth's above the air; none at nightFission for steady power, solar only with large storage; crops grown under LEDs
Radiation0.64 mSv a day on the ground, about 100 times Earth's natural doseA few metres of soil or water overhead, above all where people sleep and work
UltravioletAlmost no ozone layer to stop itGlazing and coatings that block it; plastics kept out of the sun
Toxic soilAbout 0.5% perchlorate at the Phoenix landing siteWashed out of water and farm soil; dust kept out of the rooms
MarsquakesThe largest in four years of listening: magnitude 4.7A small design load; flexible joints for pipes, seals and glazing
MeteoritesOver 200 new craters 4 m or wider every year, planet-wideThe risk to any one building is tiny, and soil cover protects
Frozen groundIce-rich soil and buried ice through the middle and high latitudesInsulated foundations, as on Earth's permafrost, so a warm building does not melt the ground under it; the ice is also the water supply
Low gravity0.38 g; what years of it do to the body is unknownDaily exercise and medical checks; loads, cranes and people carry less weight
Distance3–22 minutes by radio; a ship only every 26 monthsSelf-reliance: spare parts, medicines, local manufacturing and robots, and crews trained to handle emergencies alone
Keeping Mars cleanEarth microbes could spread to the places where Martian life might be foundCleaned spacecraft kept away from places where liquid water might occur, under the COSPAR planetary protection rules; people carry microbes, so crewed bases need new rules, now being drafted

The Pentagon answers most of them at once: 16 m of soil holds in its air and stops the radiation, its insulated walls keep the ice round it frozen, four reactors give the same power in any storm, and the storm reserve on L4 holds two years of food.
