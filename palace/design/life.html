Chapter 08

Life support

Outside, the air is a hundredth as thick as Earth's and almost all carbon dioxide, the ground is −60 °C, the soil is laced with perchlorate salts and the sky lets through cosmic rays. Inside Arcadia none of that reaches Jim. A level of machines, L4, 59 m down, makes the air and the water from Mars itself and takes them back again; the garden level grows the food; heat pumps keep it warm; and the soil and ice overhead keep the radiation out.

The air70 kPa with 27% oxygen

What the body needs is enough oxygen pressure: at sea level it is 21.2 kPa, the 20.9% of oxygen in air at 101.3 kPa. Arcadia's air is 70 kPa with 27% oxygen, which gives 18.9 kPa, a little more than in Calgary at 1,045 m. Nobody notices it, except that water boils at 90 °C, so the kitchens cook with pressure cookers (chapter 04).

Why not Earth's pressure? Every wall, window and door holds the air in against the near-vacuum outside, so lower pressure is a lighter, safer building, and less gas to make and to lose. Why not lower still? NASA's choice for exploration habitats, 56.5 kPa with 34% oxygen, needs so much oxygen in the mix that many things burn fast. At 27% the risk is closer to Earth's (see fire).

Going outside needs no waiting. The suits work at 56.5 kPa, as NASA's newest suit design can, which is more than the nitrogen and argon in Arcadia's air (51 kPa), so no one has to breathe pure oxygen first to keep the bends away.

How much air. Arcadia holds about 2.3 million m³: 2.0 million in the Pentagon's five levels, 0.1 million in the atrium and 0.15 million in the Crown and the Orb. At 70 kPa and 21 °C a cubic metre weighs 0.94 kg, so the whole fill is about 2,100 tonnes: 560 t of oxygen and 1,560 t of nitrogen and argon.

Where it comes from. The oxygen comes from water, split by electricity in the oxygen plant (L4-09), and from the fuel plant at the spaceport, which makes about 100 t of oxygen more than each ship needs (chapter 07). The nitrogen and argon, which keep the oxygen from being too strong, come from the Mars air itself: they are about 3% of it by weight, so the robots freeze the carbon dioxide out of about 52,000 t of Mars air (L4-11) in the years of building before Jim arrives (chapter 10). The tanks (L4-12) keep a reserve for the leaks of every day and the airlocks.

Breathing out. A person uses about 0.84 kg of oxygen a day and breathes out about 1 kg of carbon dioxide. The gardens on L2 take in far more than that: under their lamps they fix roughly a tonne of carbon dioxide a day (our estimate), what a thousand people breathe out. So the scrubbers (L4-10) are for the rooms, and the gardens are fed carbon dioxide from the Mars air, of which there is no end.

Real technology

Making oxygen on Mars has been done: MOXIE, on NASA's Perseverance rover, split Mars carbon dioxide into oxygen 16 times between 2021 and 2023, 122 g in all. Water electrolysis makes the oxygen on the International Space Station every day. Separating gases by freezing is how air is split into oxygen and nitrogen on Earth.

WaterFrom the ice, and back again

Plenty to start with. The ground under Arcadia is ice from 14 m down. Digging the Pentagon brought up about 1.5 million tonnes of it (chapter 03), the Crown's walls hold another 90,000 t as a radiation shield (chapter 02), and the ice mine by the spaceport adds more (chapter 07). What people use in a year is a few tonnes.

Clean water. The ice is melted in the ice melt (L4-05) with warm water from the heat pumps (L4-02), then cleaned in purification (L4-06): Mars soil holds about half a percent of perchlorate salts, which harm the thyroid, and they are taken out by ion exchange and reverse osmosis, as they are from polluted wells on Earth. Bacteria that live on perchlorate break what is left into ordinary salt and oxygen.

Used again. Every drop that goes down a drain, every breath of steam from the pools and the gardens, comes back through water recycling (L4-07), which returns 98% of it clean. So Arcadia can be generous: about 200 litres a person a day, as in a home in Canada, with deep baths and a 50 m pool, and only 2% made up from the ice.

The biggest stream is the gardens. Under the lamps the plants on L2 breathe out roughly 100 tonnes of water a day (our estimate). Coolers in the air catch it and it falls again at night as mist, and over the stream and the falls into the lake.

Real technology

The International Space Station's water system has returned 98% of its water since 2023, when a unit that dries the last brine was added. Perchlorate was found in Mars soil by the Phoenix lander in 2008, at about 0.5%; it is removed from drinking water on Earth by the same methods.

FoodThe farm under the lamps, and two years in store

The farm is on the garden level, L2, under a sky of lamps that uses 2 MW (chapter 05). Grain, rice and beans grow in fields; greens on racks in the vertical farm; vegetables and herbs in the market garden; oranges, apples, olives, figs and grapes in the orchards; fish in the fish farm and in the aquaponics, where they feed the plants; mushrooms on the garden's waste. Bees from the meadow carry the pollen.

Enough for about a hundred people. Under the farm's light, about as bright as a summer day, staple crops give roughly 35 kcal per m² a day (our estimate from NASA's crop trials, which grew more under brighter lamps). The fields, the vertical farm's racks and the aquaponics make about 7,000 m² of beds, so about 250,000 kcal a day: a hundred people at 2,500 kcal, besides the fruit, the oil and the fish. Jim's household needs a small part of it; the rest is for guests, and for the first neighbours (chapter 01).

The storm reserve. A global dust storm cannot dim the farm, which runs on the city's power under lamps, but the farm can fail like anything else: a blight, a fault. So L4 keeps two years of food (L4-13), the time to the next ship from Earth, and the seed store (L2-10) and the seed vault on L5 can start the farm again.

Nothing is wasted. Leaves, peel and scraps go to compost (L2-21) and to the waste-to-soil plant (L4-15), and come back as soil for the farm.

WarmthHeat pumps, and the ground that keeps it in

The ground round the Pentagon is about −60 °C all year, and the air round the Crown swings from −20 °C at noon to −90 °C before dawn. Inside it is 21 °C. The heat comes from heat pumps on L4 (L4-02), which take the warmth back out of the air leaving the rooms and out of the machines and put it into water pipes in the floors: about 2 MW in the coldest weeks, for about 0.7 MW of the city's power (chapter 05).

Keeping the ground frozen. Arcadia is built in ice, and ice that thaws sinks. So the Pentagon's walls, roof and floor carry thick insulation, and the outer face of the walls stays below freezing, as under buildings on permafrost in northern Canada.

Real technology

District heating from power stations warms whole cities on Earth, and buildings on permafrost in Canada, Alaska and Siberia keep the ground under them frozen with insulation and air gaps. In the thin Mars air a building loses heat mostly by conduction into the ground and by radiation, not by wind.

The gardens and the baths are kept warmer and wetter than the rooms, the archives and the stores cooler and drier, each sector on its own controls in its plant room (the residence's is L1-16).

RadiationBy day down, by night up

On the open plain, cosmic rays and the Sun's particles give about 230 mSv a year, measured by Curiosity's radiation detector: over a hundred times what a person gets on Earth from nature. Behind the Crown's 3 m walls of ice it is about 75; under the Pentagon's 16 m of soil it is about 1 (chapter 03).

So Arcadia has a rule: by day down in the Pentagon, by night up in the Crown. By day the Sun pours down ultraviolet and the particles of its storms, with no ozone layer or magnetic field to stop them; at night the planet itself stands between Jim and the Sun, and the sky he goes up for, the stars, is out. Jim spends the day below, goes up at sunset for about 6 hours of each 24 h 40 min sol, for dinners, music and the stars, and comes down to sleep. That gives him about 19 mSv a year: within the limit for radiation workers on Earth, and over 20 years less than NASA allows an astronaut in a career.

Solar storms. A large flare can send a burst of particles that gives tens of mSv in a day on the open surface. The storm watch (L3-20) sees the flare as it happens; the particles arrive minutes to hours later. Everyone goes down by portal, at most two minutes away, and stays below until it passes. The Orb's rest rooms close their windows' shutters.

A walk on the plain in a suit adds about 0.05 mSv an hour, nothing to worry about for an afternoon.

Future technology · radiation glass

The Orb's rest rooms look out through panes that stop radiation as well as the Crown's walls. Real fallback: deep windows of acrylic and water about 1.5 m thick, and resting there only part of the day, which the budget above allows for (chapter 02).

Dust and perchloratesMars stays outside

Mars dust is finer than flour, charged with static so it clings to everything, and its soil carries perchlorate salts. Breathed in, it harms the lungs; perchlorate harms the thyroid. Arcadia's answer is that the dust never comes in.

  • Suits stay outside. In the suit room (C-01) and the rover hall on L5, each suit hangs in a port in the outer wall, its back to the inside: you climb in from indoors and it seals behind you. NASA has built and tested suit ports like this on its exploration rovers.
  • The pod is cleaned before it comes in. The hangar is the airlock: brushes and jets of air take the dust off the pod before the hangar fills with air (chapter 04).
  • Filters everywhere. Every air handler has fine filters and static traps, and the robots clean the floors by the airlocks every sol.
  • Clean water. The perchlorate in the ice is taken out on L4 (L4-06) before any water reaches a tap.

The dust storms themselves do Arcadia no harm: there are no solar panels to bury, the city's power comes in on buried cables, and the farm's light comes from lamps. In a storm, shutters close over the Crown's slots and life moves below (chapter 02).

FireSafe at 27% oxygen

Fire depends mostly on how much of the air is oxygen. At 27% many things burn faster than in Earth's air, and some things that would go out by themselves keep burning. Arcadia is designed for it:

  1. No open flames. The hearths are long flames of lit mist (chapter 04); the kitchens cook on induction.
  2. Materials that pass at 27%. Stone, ceramic, glass and metal for the building; fabrics, wood and finishes chosen or treated so they don't spread a flame, tested as NASA tests the materials for spacecraft, in the air they will be used in.
  3. Seen at once. Smoke, heat and gas sensors in every room, watched by the house mind day and night.
  4. Put out with mist. Water-mist sprinklers smother a fire with little water.
  5. Kept in one sector. Pressure doors in the streets and avenues close each sector off (chapter 03), and every point has two ways out.
  6. Stores where nothing can burn. The book stacks (L1-30), the data vault (L3-15) and the spare parts (L4-14) are tended by robots in air with only 15% oxygen, where fire cannot start, as in some archives and data centres on Earth.
Real technology

NASA tests every material for its spacecraft at the oxygen level it will meet (its standard NASA-STD-6001), and chose 34% oxygen for exploration habitats only with materials that pass. Low-oxygen fire prevention keeps the air in some libraries, archives and server rooms at about 15% oxygen, enough for short visits, too little for a flame.

HealthA doctor's surgery 225 million km from the nearest hospital

The medical centre (L3-10, 913 m²) has a doctor's surgery, a scanner and an operating room, a pharmacy stocked for two years and the clean rooms next door (L3-11) to make medicines. A robot surgeon works under a doctor's eye, and doctors on Earth answer by message: a reply takes 6 to 44 minutes there and back, so the house mind and the robots handle what cannot wait. There is no evacuation: a ship to Earth leaves only every 26 months.

Low gravity. On Mars a body weighs 38% of what it does on Earth. In orbit, with no gravity at all, astronauts lose bone and muscle unless they exercise two hours a day; nobody yet knows how much 38% protects. So exercise is built into the day: the gym (C-15) and the sky pool (C-14) up in the Crown, the sports hall (L1-24) and the 50 m lap pool (L1-23) below, and the walks round the Glide and the gardens.

Sleep and light. The sky ceilings follow the Mars day of 24 h 40 min: bright and cool at noon, warm in the evening, dark at night, so the body keeps time (chapter 04).

The mind. Distance and the long wait for news weigh on people far from home. Arcadia answers with what Earth has and Mars doesn't: gardens, water and trees, views, music and books; with Earth itself, in letters and video letters and in the Orb, where Jim can stand over his home town (chapter 02); and with company when visitors come.

Future technology · the Wormhole Gate

In the design, the Gate could bring a doctor, or take Jim home, in an instant. It is a dream; the real plan is the medical centre and the two-year stores.
