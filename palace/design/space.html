Chapter 09

Communications and space

Earth is 55 to 401 million km away, and a signal at the speed of light takes 3 to 22 minutes to cross. Arcadia talks to it through three relay satellites that hang over Mars and send everything on by laser, with a fourth far out beside Earth's orbit for the weeks when the Sun is in the way. Overhead, two small moons cross the sky, Earth shines as a blue morning or evening star, and the clocks keep Mars time.

The delayMinutes away, always

Earth and Mars both go round the Sun, Earth a little faster, so every 26 months Earth overtakes Mars: then they are at their closest, and the delay is a few minutes. Half a cycle later they are on opposite sides of the Sun, and it is over 20.

No phone calls. Nobody can talk live across even 3 minutes, so Arcadia's link to Earth is for messages: letters, video letters, files, the news. Jim records a video letter in the evening; his family on Earth sees it before breakfast and answers; he has the answer the next day. Doctors, engineers and friends on Earth work the same way, a day at a time.

Behind the Sun. Every 26 months Mars passes behind the Sun as seen from Earth: for two weeks or more it is within 2° of it, and the Sun's radio noise drowns a direct signal. Missions on Mars today go quiet for those weeks. Arcadia doesn't, thanks to its fourth relay (below); the messages just take a few minutes longer.

The linksRelays over Mars, lasers to Earth

Three relays over Mars. At 17,032 km above the equator a satellite goes round once a sol, as fast as Mars turns, so it hangs still over one place: the areostationary orbit. Relay 1 hangs over the spaceport, at 202.1° E, 43° up in Arcadia's southern sky, always in view, and Arcadia talks to it by radio, which goes through dust storms. Relays 2 and 3 are spaced 120° round the orbit, so together they see all of Mars but the poles, and they pass messages to each other by laser: from the TTMath campus in Gale crater, from the spaceport, from a rover anywhere.

Lasers to Earth. Above the dust, a laser beam stays narrow over millions of km, so it carries far more than radio. In 2023 NASA's DSOC experiment on the Psyche probe sent video to Earth by laser at 267 megabits a second from 31 million km, and in 2024 at 25 megabits from 226 million km, about Mars' average distance. Arcadia's relays carry telescopes 1 m across instead of DSOC's 22 cm, enough for about 200 megabits a second when Mars is farthest and over a gigabit when it is close (our estimate): a video letter goes in under a minute. Radio to NASA's Deep Space Network on Earth is the slower back-up.

Round the Sun. The fourth relay, Arcadia Relay 4, goes round the Sun on Earth's orbit, 60° ahead of Earth, at the place where the Sun's and Earth's pulls balance (the L4 point). When the Sun comes between Earth and Mars, the relay still sees Mars 37° from the Sun, and Earth 60° from it, so the link goes round. The messages take about 27 minutes then.

On the ground. Arcadia's antennas are flat panels set into the white roofs of the five corner pavilions (chapter 01), each looking up at Relay 1: any one is enough, so one can be cleaned or mended while the others work. A second set is on the spaceport's 77 m control tower, joined to Arcadia by the optical fibre in the cable trench (chapter 05). Below ground, the Earth link (L3-16) runs them, beside the house mind (L3-14) and its back-up (L3-18), the data vault (L3-15) and the city's control room (L3-19).

Real technology

Mars orbiters relay the rovers' data to Earth every day, and the areostationary orbit is well understood; nobody has put a satellite there yet. Laser links to deep space were proved by DSOC in 2023–2024. Relays at Sun–Earth L4 or L5 have been studied for exactly this, to keep Mars in touch through conjunction.

Living with the delayEarth by letter, and a library of it at home

  • Letters and video letters every day, recorded anywhere: in the study (C-23, L1-04), by the fire, in the memory rooms (L1-14), where Jim records his memoirs for his family.
  • The news, a few minutes old, in the Earth lounge in the Orb (O-07), where the universe zooms down to Earth and his home town with its weather (chapter 02), and on the screens in every room.
  • Concerts and talks streamed from Earth to the cinema (L1-17), a few minutes after the fact.
  • Earth at home. The Archive of Earth (L1-28) and the film and music archive (L1-29) keep the world's books, films, music and the family's records on Mars, so nothing waits for the link; a sealed copy lies in the vault on L5 (L5-11).
  • Help that can't wait comes from Arcadia itself: the house mind, the medical centre and the robots (chapter 08). Earth's experts answer within the hour.

The link also runs Arcadia's own business with Earth: the ships' schedules from flight control (L3-21), the weather and radiation warnings from the storm watch (L3-20), and the house mind's updates. Nothing that keeps Arcadia alive depends on Earth answering in time.

Future technology · the Wormhole Gate

In the design, the Gate in the Orb sends people and things anywhere instantly (chapter 02), but it carries no messages faster than light: the relays and the lasers are how Arcadia really talks to Earth.

The skyTwo moons, and Earth as a star

Phobos, 27 km long, goes round Mars in 7 h 39 min, faster than Mars turns, so it rises in the west, crosses the southern sky in about four hours and sets in the east, about twice a sol. It looks about a third as wide as the Moon from Earth. In some seasons it crosses the Sun: a black shape slides over it in less than a minute.

Deimos, 15 km long, goes round in 30 h 18 min, only a little slower than Mars turns, so it rises in the east and takes more than two sols to cross the sky: a bright star, not a disc.

Earth is an inner planet seen from Mars, so it is never far from the Sun: a blue evening star after sunset for months, then a morning star before dawn. When Jim lands in 2027 it is the morning star. Through the Observatory's telescope (C-30 to C-32) the Moon shows beside it.

The Sun looks two thirds as wide as from Earth, and sets in a blue glow (chapter 02). From the Crown, at night, the Milky Way is as clear as from a desert on Earth.

Mars timeA sol of 24 h 40 min, a year of 668 sols

The sol, Mars' day, is 24 h 39 min 35 s. Arcadia's clocks count 24 hours to the sol, each 2.7% longer than an Earth hour, so the extra 40 minutes are spread through the day and nobody notices them. Arcadia keeps local mean solar time for its longitude, 201.44° E, worked out the way NASA's Mars24 clock does it, so the sky ceilings, meals and sleep follow the real sun outside (chapter 04).

A Mars clock in every room shows Mars time and the sol number beside the time in Jim's home town on Earth, so he knows when his family is awake.

The year is 668.6 sols, 687 Earth days. Its seasons are unequal, because Mars' orbit is more oval than Earth's: at Arcadia, 39.8° north, spring and summer last 372 sols and autumn and winter only 297. The great dust storms come in the southern summer, when Mars is closest to the Sun.

Scientists count Mars years from 1955: Mars Year 39 began on 30 September 2026, and Jim lands in its northern summer, in mid-2027.
