Dust storms

Weeks

Local storms rise every year, mostly in the southern spring and summer, when Mars is nearest the Sun. Every few Mars years one spreads round the whole planet for weeks: in 2018 one darkened the sky over the rover Opportunity until its solar panels could no longer keep it alive, after 14 years of work.

Dust devils

20 km

Whirlwinds of dust cross the plains on spring and summer afternoons. One photographed from orbit in Amazonis Planitia, next to Arcadia, stood about 20 km high and only some 60 m wide. Their gusts have swept rovers' solar panels clean, giving Spirit and Opportunity years of extra life.

More on the weather →

Wind

1.6%

The air is 1.6% as dense as Earth's, so a 100 km/h storm wind pushes no harder than a 13 km/h breeze at home. Wind barely loads a building, but the dust and sand it carries scour surfaces, and orbiters have watched sand dunes creep across the plains.

More on the weather →

Cold

−125 °C

Thin air holds little heat. A summer afternoon near the equator reaches about 20 °C at the ground, and the same night falls below −80 °C. Polar winter nights reach −125 °C, cold enough for carbon dioxide to freeze out of the air as frost.

The air and the cold →

Clouds, fog, frost and snow

Snow

Thin clouds of water ice, and higher up of carbon-dioxide ice; morning fog in the canyons; frost on the ground in winter. The Phoenix lander's laser watched snow falling from clouds at night, and on the poles carbon-dioxide snow falls in winter.

More on the weather →

Seasons

2×

Mars tilts 25°, close to Earth's 23°, so it has seasons, each about twice as long. Its oval orbit brings 45% more sunlight at its nearest to the Sun than at its farthest: southern summers are short and hot, northern ones long and mild. Each winter the pole freezes about a quarter of the air out of the sky.

The planet

Geography

21.9 km

The north is a smooth lowland, the south old highland crowded with craters, a few kilometres higher. Olympus Mons rises about 22 km, two and a half times Everest; the canyons of Valles Marineris run over 4,000 km and drop 7 km; the Hellas basin is 2,300 km across.

Water and ice

20 m

No liquid water lasts on the surface: at this pressure ice turns straight to vapour. But there is ice nearly everywhere. The polar caps hold enough to cover the planet about 20 m deep, and radar shows clean buried ice tens of metres thick across the northern plains, Arcadia Planitia among the richest.

A wet past

Rivers and lakes

Dry riverbeds, deltas and lake beds show that more than three billion years ago, under thicker air, water flowed on Mars. Curiosity has driven across the mud of an old lake in Gale crater, where demo 1 stands; Perseverance is collecting rock samples from a river delta in Jezero crater.

The soil

Basalt

Crushed volcanic rock, coloured red by rusty iron oxides, with salts such as sulfates and perchlorates. Its dust is as fine as smoke, a few thousandths of a millimetre, and hangs in the air. It is also the main building material.

Sky and light

Blue sunsets

By day the dusty sky is butterscotch. At sunset the fine dust scatters blue light forwards, so the sky round the Sun turns blue, as the rovers have photographed. The Sun looks about two-thirds as wide as from Earth.

The air and the dust →

Two moons

7 h 39 min

Phobos, about 22 km across, circles Mars in 7 h 39 min, faster than Mars turns, so it rises in the west twice a day; tides will pull it apart into a ring in 30 to 50 million years. Deimos, about 12 km across, looks like a bright star.

Hazards

Thin air

636 Pa

0.6% of Earth's air pressure, far below 6.3 kPa, where water boils at body temperature. Outside, people need a full pressure suit, not just an oxygen mask; inside, every building is a sealed pressure vessel.

Designing for it →

Radiation

0.64 mSv a day

Mars lost its global magnetic field about four billion years ago, and its air is a hundred times thinner than ours, so cosmic rays and bursts of particles from the Sun reach the ground. Curiosity measured about 100 times Earth's natural dose. A few metres of soil stops most of it.

The radiation →

Ultraviolet

No ozone

Mars has almost no ozone layer, so the hard ultraviolet that ours blocks reaches the ground. It sterilises exposed surfaces, all the more with the perchlorate salts in the soil, and breaks down plastics and paints left outside.

The hazards →

Toxic dust

0.5%

The soil at the Phoenix landing site was about half a percent perchlorate, a salt that upsets the thyroid. It must be washed out of water and farm soil, and the fine dust that clings to suits by static kept out of the rooms and the lungs.

The hazards →

Marsquakes

M 4.7

NASA's InSight lander listened from 2018 to 2022 and recorded more than 1,300 marsquakes; the largest, in May 2022, was magnitude 4.7. Mars shakes far less than Earth, so quakes are a small load for its buildings.

The hazards →

Meteorites

200+ a year

The thin air burns up fewer meteoroids: more than 200 new craters at least 4 m wide form every year. In December 2021 one dug a crater 150 m wide in Amazonis Planitia, felt by InSight as a magnitude 4 quake, and threw out boulders of buried ice. A hit on any one building is very unlikely.

How it is done

Getting there

26 months

Earth and Mars line up for an efficient trip once every 26 months, and the trip takes six to nine months. Landing is the hard part: the heaviest lander so far, Perseverance, is about a tonne. A settlement needs loads of a hundred tonnes, what SpaceX's Starship is designed to land; it has not yet flown to Mars.

Getting there →

Bring little, make the rest

ISRU

Bring what Mars cannot make: machines, electronics, reactors, medicines, seeds. Make the heavy, simple things there: water, air, rocket fuel, bricks and glass, later iron from the soil. Engineers call it in-situ resource utilisation. Robots land first and start the plants, so air, water and power are running when people arrive.

Building with soil

50 MPa

Tested on Earth with copies of Mars soil: heated to about 1,100 °C it fuses into a ceramic; mixed with melted sulfur, which Mars has, it sets into a concrete that needs no water and reached about 50 MPa, as strong as good concrete; robots can 3D-print it into walls, as in NASA's habitat challenge of 2015–2019.

Building →

Holding the air in

11 m

The biggest load on a Mars building is the air inside. At 70 kPa, the pressure 3,000 m up a mountain on Earth, it pushes up on each square metre of roof like 7 tonnes. A metre of Mars soil presses down with 6.3 kPa, so 11 m of soil balances it, and stops the radiation too: that is why so many designs are buried.

Building →

Water

98%

Dig ice-rich soil and bake the water out, or melt a well deep in clean ice, as the South Pole station does. Warming and melting a tonne of ice from −60 °C takes about 130 kWh (our sum). Take out the perchlorates, then use it again and again: the Space Station recovers 98% of its water.

Water →

Air

12 g an hour

A person breathes about 0.8 kg of oxygen a day. MOXIE, on the rover Perseverance, made oxygen from Mars air: 122 g in 16 runs from 2021 to 2023, up to 12 g an hour at 98% purity, the first made on another planet. Splitting water works too: 1 kg gives 0.89 kg of oxygen.

Air →

Food

40–50 m²

Grown indoors under LEDs, mostly with the roots in water rather than soil. NASA's studies found that 40 to 50 m² of high-yield crops can feed one person. Crops have grown in copies of Mars soil once nutrients were added; its salts must be washed out first. The lamps take about 100 kWh a day per person fed (our estimate).

Food →

Energy

Fission

Solar panels get 43% of Earth's sunlight, nothing at night and a few percent in a global dust storm; wind turbines barely turn in such thin air. A fission reactor works in any weather: NASA tested the small KRUSTY reactor in 2018 and aims to put a 100 kW reactor on the Moon by about 2030. Its waste heat warms the buildings.

Energy →

Shielding

A few metres

Light atoms stop space radiation best without making more of it: water, plastics and soil. On the way, a ship can wrap its water and food round a shelter for solar storms. On the ground, a few metres of soil take away most of the dose, so people sleep and work under cover.

Shielding →

Rocket fuel

CH₄ + O₂

Both halves of a methane rocket's fuel can be made on Mars from ice and air: split water into hydrogen and oxygen, then join the hydrogen to carbon dioxide over a nickel catalyst, the Sabatier reaction, used on the Space Station since 2010. A ship fuelled on Mars need not carry its fuel home from Earth.

Rocket fuel →

Health

1–1.5% a month

In weightlessness astronauts lose 1 to 1.5% of their hip and spine bone a month without hard exercise; what 0.38 g does over years nobody knows. Medicine must manage without evacuation. Isolation has been tested on Earth: Mars-500 sealed six men in for 520 days, and NASA's CHAPEA crew lived 378 days in a 3D-printed mock Mars home.

Health →

Talking to Earth

3–22 min

Radio takes 3 to 22 minutes each way, so there are no live calls: messages, video letters and email. Every 26 months the Sun passes between the planets and cuts contact for about two weeks. Lasers carry far more than radio: in 2023 NASA's DSOC sent video by laser from 31 million km away.
