Hazards

What on Mars can kill you, and how badly: the thin air, the cold, radiation from space, ultraviolet, toxic dust, dust storms, marsquakes and meteorites. The first three are the serious ones; the last two are small. How each is answered is in Building on Mars.

Thin airNot just unbreathable

The air presses down with 636 Pa, 0.6% of Earth's. That is far below 6.3 kPa, the pressure at which water boils at body temperature, so an unprotected person would not just suffocate: the water in their mouth and eyes would boil away, and they would lose consciousness within seconds. Outside, people need a full pressure suit, not just an oxygen mask; inside, every building is a sealed pressure vessel.

Cold−63 °C on average

A summer afternoon near the equator reaches about 20 °C at the ground; the night falls below −80 °C, and polar winter nights reach −125 °C. Machines need heaters to survive the night, and every material outside expands and shrinks with the daily swing. See weather.

RadiationNo magnetic field, and little air

Earth's magnetic field and thick air stop most of the particles that fly through space: cosmic rays from distant exploding stars, and bursts of particles from the Sun. Mars lost its global magnetic field about four billion years ago (see inside Mars), and its air is a hundred times thinner, so they reach the ground.

Curiosity's radiation detector measured about 0.64 mSv a day on the ground in Gale crater, about 100 times Earth's natural dose, and 1.8 mSv a day on the way there. A trip of six months each way with 500 days on Mars adds up to about 1 sievert, more than NASA's career limit for an astronaut, 600 mSv.

The answer is mass overhead: a few metres of soil stop most of it. See shielding.

UltravioletNo ozone layer

Earth's ozone layer stops the harshest ultraviolet light from the Sun. Mars has almost no ozone, so ultraviolet down to wavelengths of about 200 nanometres reaches the ground. It sterilises exposed surfaces, and laboratory work shows that the perchlorate salts in the soil make it deadlier still to bacteria. It also breaks down plastics, paints and fabrics left in the sun. Ordinary glass blocks it.

Toxic dustFine, clinging and salty

The Phoenix lander found perchlorate salts making up about half a percent of the soil, and Curiosity has found them in Gale crater: they are harmful to people because they block the thyroid's uptake of iodine. The dust is as fine as smoke, clings to suits by static and works into seals, joints and lungs. On the Moon, the Apollo crews found that clinging dust scratched visors, clogged seals and irritated their eyes and lungs; Mars dust is less sharp, worn by the wind, but it carries the salts.

Dust stormsWeeks without much sun

A global dust storm can darken the sky for weeks and cut the sunlight reaching the ground to a few percent. It is not dangerous by its wind, which barely pushes, but by the darkness: anything that runs on solar power starves. The 2018 storm ended the Opportunity rover. See weather.

MarsquakesA small hazard

NASA's InSight lander recorded 1,319 marsquakes from 2018 to 2022; the largest, on 4 May 2022, was magnitude 4.7. Mars is far quieter than Earth, so quakes are a small load for a building. See inside Mars.

MeteoritesMore reach the ground, but rarely

The thin air burns up fewer meteoroids than Earth's: more than 200 new craters at least 4 m wide form across Mars each year. On 24 December 2021 one dug a crater 150 m wide in Amazonis Planitia, close to Arcadia; InSight felt it as a magnitude 4 quake, and orbiters saw boulder-sized blocks of ice thrown out. Spread over the whole planet, the chance of a hit on any one building is very small, and soil on top protects.
