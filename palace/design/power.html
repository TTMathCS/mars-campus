Chapter 05

Power

On Mars, power is life: it makes the air, melts the water, lights the farms and fuels the ships home. Sunlight is weak, a global dust storm can hide it for weeks, and Jim wants no panels spread over the ground, so Arcadia and the spaceport run on nuclear power that never stops.

What needs powerDemand in phase 1

The biggest single user is light: the farms and gardens on L2 grow under lamps. The second is the fuel plant at the spaceport, which turns ice and air into methane and oxygen for the ships and pods (chapter 07). Everything else, the air and water plants, the rooms, the foundry and the port, adds up to about a third.

The fuel plant is the one load that can wait. It takes whatever the rest of the grid is not using, so the reactors run steadily, and it slows down when a reactor is being refuelled.

Future technology

The Crown's anti-gravity drives and the portals have no known physics, so their power needs are guesses. The design keeps 0.5 MW for them and has room to grow.

Where it comes fromFour reactors, and nothing on the ground

Fission microreactors. Four units of 5 MWe each, the size of a shipping container, cooled by heat pipes with no pumps to fail. Two sit in the power sector of the Pentagon's L4, 59 m down; two sit in a buried vault at the spaceport. Each runs for about 8 years before it is refuelled. Reactors like this are being developed on Earth, and NASA is developing reactors to power bases on the Moon.

Batteries of 20 MWh at Arcadia and 20 MWh at the port cover a reactor trip or the evening peak. Fuel cells can burn the methane and oxygen stored for the ships: 1,000 tonnes of propellant keeps 8 MW going for about 9 days.

Real technology

Heat-pipe microreactors of about 5 MWe are in development on Earth; NASA's Kilopower test (KRUSTY, 2018) ran a small heat-pipe reactor at full power for 28 hours, and NASA's Fission Surface Power project is developing reactors for the Moon. Solar power on Mars is proven too: Spirit, Opportunity and InSight ran on it. But a field big enough for Arcadia would cover the plain in panels, and a global dust storm can hide the sun for weeks; one ended Opportunity in 2018. So Arcadia runs on reactors alone.

The gridOne cable, two ends

A buried direct-current cable runs 30 km beside the rover road, at ±20 kV, able to carry 15 MW either way; at full load about 1.5% is lost on the way, less in the cold ground. With it, Arcadia and the spaceport act as one grid: the reactors at either end can carry the other's loads, so one can be refuelled or repaired while the lights stay on.

An optical fibre runs in the same trench for data, as the backup to the radio links (chapter 09).

Inside Arcadia, power is distributed as direct current too, because the batteries and most machines work on DC. The switchgear is on L4 next to the reactors.

HeatWarmth for Arcadia, the rest to the sky

A reactor makes about twice as much heat as electricity. Arcadia uses some of it: warm water from the turbines heats the Pentagon's floors, melts ice for water and keeps the gardens warm, about 2 MW in all.

The rest, up to 20 MW, goes to the radiator field 400 m north of Arcadia: 150 × 60 m of tall vertical fins that glow faintly warm against the −60 °C sky. The thin air carries little heat away, so the fins shed it mostly as infrared light. The spaceport's two reactors have their own smaller radiators.

The radiators stand north of Arcadia, out of the views from the Crown's main rooms, and far enough away that their heat doesn't thaw the ground under the Pentagon.
