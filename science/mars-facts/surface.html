The surface

Two halves, the tallest volcano and the longest canyons known, deep basins and white poles; dry riverbeds and lake beds from a wetter past; red soil, a butterscotch sky and blue sunsets.

Two halvesLow smooth north, high cratered south

The south is old highland, crowded with craters; the north is a smooth plain a few kilometres lower, covering about a third of the planet. Why Mars is split this way is still argued: one giant impact early in its history, or the way its mantle churned. The heights were measured by the laser altimeter on Mars Global Surveyor, which mapped the whole planet to within a few metres.

Volcanoes and canyonsThe tallest and the longest known

Tharsis and Olympus Mons. A volcanic plateau thousands of kilometres across carries three giant volcanoes in a row, and Olympus Mons to the north-west: the tallest volcano known in the solar system, about 22 km above the planet's average level and some 600 km wide. They grew so tall because Mars has no moving plates, so the same hot spots fed them for a very long time, and its weaker gravity lets mountains stand higher.

Valles Marineris. A system of canyons more than 4,000 km long, up to 200 km wide and 7 km deep: as long as the United States is wide, and about four times as deep as the Grand Canyon. It is a crack in the crust, opened as Tharsis rose, and widened since by landslides.

Basins and polesDeep scars and white caps

Hellas. An impact basin 2,300 km across and about 7 km deep. Its floor is the lowest place on Mars, where the air pressure is nearly twice the average: about 1,150 Pa. Argyre, 1,800 km across, is another giant impact scar in the south.

The poles. White caps of water ice up to about 3 km thick, layered like tree rings by changes in climate, with a winter coat of frozen carbon dioxide. Each winter so much carbon dioxide freezes onto the pole that the air pressure over the whole planet drops by about a quarter. Radar from orbit has measured how much ice the caps hold: see resources.

A wet pastDry rivers, deltas and lake beds

Mars is covered in dry riverbeds, deltas and lake beds from more than three billion years ago, when its air was thicker and water could flow. Two rovers are reading that history in the rocks:

  • Curiosity has driven since 2012 across the mud, now rock, of an old lake in Gale crater. At Yellowknife Bay it found that the lake was once fit for microbes: fresh water with the chemical energy and elements that life on Earth uses. It has also found organic molecules preserved in mudstones three billion years old. Demo 1's TTMath campus stands on Curiosity's ground.
  • Perseverance landed in 2021 in Jezero crater, where a river once built a delta into a lake. It is collecting rock samples in sealed tubes that may one day be brought back to Earth.

No life has been found. If microbes ever lived on Mars, the best places to look for their traces are these old lake beds, and underground, out of the radiation.

The soilCrushed volcanic rock, coloured by rust

Mars soil is crushed basalt, the dark volcanic rock of Iceland and Hawaii: grains of olivine, pyroxene and feldspar, with iron oxides, rust in effect, that make the planet red, and salts such as sulfates and perchlorates. The rovers have also found clays, which form only in water.

The finest part is dust, a few thousandths of a millimetre across, as fine as smoke. It hangs in the air, settles on everything and clings by static.

What it means for people

The soil is the main building material, and a shield against radiation; its perchlorate salts are harmful and must be washed out of water and farm soil. See resources, hazards and construction.

Sky and lightButterscotch days and blue sunsets

By day the dusty sky is butterscotch. At sunset the fine dust scatters blue light forwards, so the sky round the setting Sun turns blue, as the rovers have photographed. The Sun looks about two-thirds as wide as it does from Earth, and gives 43% of Earth's sunlight; Earth itself is a bright evening or morning star (see Mars in space).

One placeArcadia Planitia

A smooth, icy plain of the northern lowlands, west of the Tharsis volcanoes and north of Amazonis Planitia. It lies about 3.9 km below the planet's average level, so the air there is about 40% thicker than average, 870 Pa. The ground is flat and nearly free of boulders, and radar and fresh craters show clean ice tens of metres thick, within a metre or so of the surface over much of the plain. NASA's study of buried ice in the northern plains names the region among the best places for people to find water.
