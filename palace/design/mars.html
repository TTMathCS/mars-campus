Chapter 00·1 · The science

Mars, the planet

Before the house, the planet it stands on. Mars is half as wide as Earth, with a bit over a third of its gravity, a day 40 minutes longer, air less than a hundredth as thick, and cold and radiation no one could live in unprotected. It also has ice, sunlight, carbon dioxide and soil: everything needed to make water, air, fuel and buildings. Every number here was measured by spacecraft; the sources are at the end.

GeographyTwo halves, giant volcanoes, a great canyon and a deep basin

Two halves. The south is old highland, crowded with craters; the north is a smooth plain a few kilometres lower, covering about a third of the planet. Why Mars is split this way is still argued: one giant impact early on, or the way its mantle churned.

Tharsis and Olympus Mons. A volcanic plateau thousands of kilometres across carries three giant volcanoes in a row, and Olympus Mons to the north-west: the tallest volcano known in the solar system, about 22 km above the planet's average level and some 600 km wide. They grew so tall because Mars has no moving plates, so the same hot spots fed them for a very long time, and its weaker gravity lets mountains stand higher.

Valles Marineris. A system of canyons more than 4,000 km long, up to 200 km wide and 7 km deep: as long as the United States is wide, and about four times as deep as the Grand Canyon.

Hellas. An impact basin 2,300 km across and about 7 km deep. Its floor is the lowest place on Mars, where the air pressure is nearly twice the average.

The poles. White caps of water ice up to about 3 km thick, with a winter coat of frozen carbon dioxide. Each winter so much carbon dioxide freezes onto the pole that the air pressure over the whole planet drops by about a quarter.

A wet past. Mars is covered in dry riverbeds, deltas and lake beds from more than three billion years ago, when its air was thicker. Curiosity has driven across the mud of an old lake in Gale crater, where demo 1's TTMath campus stands; Perseverance is studying a river delta in Jezero crater, sealing rock samples for a future return to Earth.

The air is less than 1% as thick as Earth's. A strong storm wind of 100 km/h pushes on you no harder than a breeze at home, because the air has about 1.6% of Earth's density. Its carbon dioxide, nitrogen and argon are all useful: carbon dioxide for oxygen and fuel, nitrogen and argon to dilute the oxygen inside, as nitrogen does on Earth.

Cold. A summer afternoon near the equator reaches about 20 °C at the ground; the same night falls below −80 °C, because thin air holds little heat. Polar winter nights reach −125 °C, cold enough for carbon dioxide to freeze out as frost.

Dust. Mars dust is as fine as smoke, a few thousandths of a millimetre, and it hangs in the air, colouring the sky butterscotch by day. At sunset the fine dust scatters blue light forwards, so the sky round the setting sun turns blue: the blue sunsets seen from the Crown are real, photographed by the rovers.

Why there is no liquid water

At about 600 Pa, the triple point of water, ice turns straight into vapour, and any water that melts boils away soon after. Liquid water can only last briefly in the lowest places, like the bottom of Hellas.

Wind. The air has about 1.6% of the density of Earth's, and the push of wind goes with density times speed squared: a 30 m/s storm wind, about 100 km/h, presses no harder than a 13 km/h breeze at home. It still lifts dust and moves sand: orbiters have watched sand dunes and ripples creep across the ground.

Dust devils. On spring and summer afternoons the sun-warmed ground sets whirlwinds spinning, and their dark tracks criss-cross the plains in photographs from orbit. One photographed in 2012 in Amazonis Planitia, next to Arcadia, stood about 20 km high and only some 60 m wide. Their gusts have swept rovers' solar panels clean, giving Spirit and Opportunity years of extra life.

Dust storms. Local storms come every year, mostly in the southern spring and summer, when Mars is nearest the Sun. Every few Mars years one grows to cover the whole planet for weeks: Mariner 9 arrived in one in 1971, and the 2018 storm darkened the sky so much that the Opportunity rover's solar panels could no longer keep it alive, after 14 years of work.

Clouds, fog, frost and snow. Thin clouds of water ice form over the volcanoes and the poles, and much higher up, clouds of carbon-dioxide ice; fog fills canyons in the morning. Viking 2 photographed frost on the ground in winter at 48° N. The Phoenix lander's laser, at 68° N, watched snow falling from clouds at night, and on the poles carbon-dioxide snow falls in winter.

Seasons. Mars tilts 25°, so it has seasons, each about twice as long as Earth's. Its orbit is more oval than Earth's, so it gets 45% more sunlight at its nearest to the Sun than at its farthest: the southern summer is short and hot, the northern one, at the house, long and mild. Each winter so much carbon dioxide freezes onto the pole that the air pressure over the whole planet drops by about a quarter, and returns in spring.

WaterIce everywhere, liquid nowhere

The polar caps hold about 3 million km³ of water ice: enough to cover the whole planet about 20 m deep.

Buried ice lies under much of the middle latitudes too, out of sight under a layer of dust. In 2008 the Phoenix lander scraped a trench and found ice a few centimetres down at 68° N; it turned to vapour in the sunlight over a few days.

Arcadia Planitia is one of the richest places: radar from orbit (SHARAD) and the terraced walls of fresh craters show clean ice tens of metres thick across a large area, starting close to the surface. NASA's Subsurface Water Ice Mapping project names the northern plains around it among the best places for people to find water. That is why the house is here.

In the air there is almost none: about 0.02% water vapour.

RadiationNo magnetic field, and little air

Earth's magnetic field and thick air stop most of the particles that fly through space: cosmic rays from distant exploding stars and bursts of particles from the Sun. Mars lost its global magnetic field about four billion years ago, and its air is a hundred times thinner, so they reach the ground.

Curiosity's radiation detector measured about 0.64 mSv a day on the ground in Gale crater, and 1.8 mSv a day on the way there. A trip of six months each way with 500 days on Mars adds up to about 1 sievert, more than NASA's career limit for an astronaut, 600 mSv.

The answer is soil. A few metres stop most of it; under 16 m of soil, the house receives less than people do on Earth.

Other hazardsUltraviolet, toxic dust, marsquakes and meteorites

Ultraviolet. Earth's ozone layer stops the harshest ultraviolet light from the Sun. Mars has almost no ozone, so ultraviolet down to wavelengths of about 200 nanometres reaches the ground. It sterilises exposed surfaces, and laboratory work shows that the perchlorate salts in the soil make it deadlier still to bacteria. It also breaks down plastics, paints and fabrics left in the sun.

Toxic dust. The Phoenix lander found perchlorate salts making up about half a percent of the soil, and Curiosity has found them in Gale crater: they are harmful to people because they block the thyroid's uptake of iodine. The dust is as fine as smoke, clings to suits by static and works into seals, joints and lungs. On the Moon, the Apollo crews found that clinging dust scratched visors, clogged seals and irritated their eyes and lungs; Mars dust is less sharp, worn by the wind, but it carries the salts.

Marsquakes. NASA's InSight lander set a seismometer on the ground in Elysium Planitia and listened from 2018 to 2022. It recorded 1,319 marsquakes; the largest, on 4 May 2022, was magnitude 4.7. Mars has no moving plates: its quakes come from faults in a planet that is slowly cooling, and from meteorite impacts. It is far quieter than Earth, so quakes are a small load for a building. The quakes also let scientists see inside another planet for the first time: its crust, its mantle and its liquid core.

Meteorites. The thin air burns up fewer meteoroids than Earth's: more than 200 new craters at least 4 m wide form across Mars each year. On 24 December 2021 one dug a crater 150 m wide in Amazonis Planitia, close to Arcadia; InSight felt it as a magnitude 4 quake, and orbiters saw boulder-sized blocks of ice thrown out, the closest to the equator that ice has been seen on Mars. The chance of a hit on any one building is very small, and soil on top protects.

The moonsPhobos and Deimos

Phobos is a dark, lumpy moon 27 by 22 by 18 km, only 6,000 km above the ground. It circles Mars in 7 h 39 min, faster than Mars turns, so it rises in the west and sets in the east, twice a day. Tides are pulling it slowly down: in about 30 to 50 million years it will break up into a ring.

Phobos often passes in front of the Sun, a quick partial eclipse that the rovers have filmed: the moment the scenic flight's sunset is timed for.

Deimos is smaller, 15 by 12 by 11 km, and 20,000 km up. It takes 30 hours to go round, so it seems to hang in the sky for days; from the ground it looks like a bright star.

Both may be pieces of a giant impact on Mars, or asteroids it captured. They are possible stepping stones: Phobos' gravity is less than a thousandth of Earth's, so landing on it and leaving again take almost no fuel.

The siteArcadia Planitia, where the house stands

A smooth, icy plain of the northern lowlands, west of the Tharsis volcanoes. The house is at 39.80° N, 201.44° E, 3.9 km below the planet's average level, so the air there is 40% thicker than average: 870 Pa. The ground is flat and nearly free of boulders, and ice lies within a metre of the surface over much of the plain.

The spaceport is on landing site AP-1, 30 km east, rated in Starship landing-site studies as the safest in Arcadia.
