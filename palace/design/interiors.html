Chapter 04

Interiors

The rooms of Arcadia, what they look like and what they are made of. The Crown is for the evenings: the sunset, the views, dinners, music and guests; the Pentagon for the day, for sleep, water, gardens and work. Everywhere the aim is the same: calm rooms of real materials that feel like Earth, in a home that could only be on Mars.

Design languageEarth materials, Mars light

Arcadia uses a small palette of real materials, most of them made on Mars: stone and ceramic from the ground, glass from its sand, metals from its rocks, wood from the trees on L2. Surfaces are matt and warm; colour comes from the materials themselves, never from paint.

Light sets the mood. In the Crown, where Jim spends the evenings, it comes through the windows at eye level: a blade of gold at sunset, then lamps and the stars. In the Pentagon every room has a sky ceiling, an artificial skylight that shows a sun and a blue sky with real optics. Products that do this exist on Earth today. It follows the time of day and can show Mars, Earth or the stars.

Water and plants are in almost every room, for the air, the sound and the smell. Quiet comes free: 3 m walls in the Crown and 16 m of soil over the Pentagon keep out every outside sound.

No room shows its machines. Air comes in through slots along the floor and leaves through the ceiling edge; power, data and water run in the walls; robots use their own service corridors behind the rooms.

The Gate, the portals, the universe in VR and the radiation glass are dreams, each with a real fallback where one is needed (chapter 02). The Gate's first destination is the TTMath campus in Gale crater.

Two master suitesOne up for mornings, one down for nights

Jim asked for two master suites, one up and one down. They sit one above the other in the south-east: the Crown's suite (C-06 to C-08) in the dip between the Arrival and Salon spires, and the Pentagon's (L1-06 to L1-09) in ring B of sector 1 on L1, 65 m below.

Master suite up · the Crown

Three rooms along the outer wall of the ring: the dressing room, the bedroom and the bath. The grand bed faces the south-east windows: Jim's room for the evening and the night up in the sky, to rest after dinner and read under the stars before going down. The walls are lined in pale oak and walnut and the floor is linen-coloured stone; the bath has a soaking tub by the windows. The portals in the Arrival and Salon spires are a short ride along the Glide.

Master suite down · L1

In ring B of sector 1, round a moss garden open to a lit roof 8 m up: the bath, the moss garden, the bedroom and the dressing room behind it, with the laundry at one end and the kitchen at the other. The bedroom's glass wall faces the moss garden, under a sky ceiling that dims to stars at night and brightens slowly before waking. Under 16 m of soil it is the quietest, safest room on Mars, and where Jim sleeps (chapter 08). Ring A next door holds the music room, the family room and the dining room, with the study upstairs.

Coming homeFrom the pod to the Arrival hall

The hangar takes the pod straight in from the garden side. The door closes, and the hangar fills with air in about 90 seconds while fans and brushes blow the dust off the pod. Then the pod's door opens onto a floor of warm stone.

The suit room beside it is for trips outside on foot. Suits hang in ports in the outer wall and never come in: you climb into a suit from inside and it seals behind you, so no Mars dust comes in. NASA has built and tested suit ports like this for rovers.

The Door is a round opening 5 m across at the back of the hangar, closed by an iris of light. It recognises you by face, eyes and the way you walk, and opens only for Jim and his guests.

The Arrival hall is the first room: 20 m tall under its spire, a basalt floor, a long olive-wood bench, and the first window slot, looking back across the garden to the Orb. From here the Glide leads round the ring, and a portal leads down to L1.

Light through the solA day of 24 h 40 min

A Mars day, a sol, lasts 24 hours 40 minutes, so Arcadia keeps Mars time and the sky ceilings follow the real sun. People adapt to the extra 40 minutes easily; Mars rover teams have lived on Mars time for months.

The ceilings follow the pattern our bodies expect: warm light at waking, bright cool light at midday, warm and dim in the evening, then darkness and stars. Bright blue-rich light in the morning sets the body clock; amber light at night lets you sleep.

In the Crown the real sun comes through the slots, and the rooms' own lamps only fill in. In a dust storm, when the sky outside turns dark for weeks, Arcadia keeps the same bright sols indoors.
